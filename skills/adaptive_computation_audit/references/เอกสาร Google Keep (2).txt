## Executive conclusion


**The central missing quantity is not “completion,” state movement, or difficulty. It is the conditional value of additional computation.**


For an information set $\mathcal F_l$ available at stage , the relevant question is:


$$
\text{How much expected task loss can continuing reduce, relative to its cost?}
$$


Most of the reported failures arise from substituting something easier to measure—confidence, residual size, a bounded resistance signal, or a soft halting distribution—for that quantity.


However, **there is no general solution satisfying all the stated requirements exactly**. Three obstacles must be separated:


1. **Semantic incompatibility:** a weighted mixture of intermediate states is generally not the same computation as deterministic first-crossing inference.
2. **Non-identifiability:** local, label-free state geometry cannot universally determine whether additional computation will improve task performance.
3. **Unavoidable preferences:** choosing an accuracy–compute tradeoff requires a budget, risk tolerance, or utility specification. Normalization cannot eliminate that choice.


These are structural issues, not evidence that adaptive computation itself is futile.


### Evidence qualification


I treat the supplied numerical results as **reported experimental observations**, not independently verified measurements. Without sample counts, uncertainty estimates, metric definitions, and matched controls, they support diagnoses and testable hypotheses—not universal conclusions.


The historical failure table also needs qualification:
- ACT explicitly proposed deterministic, differentiable adaptive computation; particular instability observations are not an impossibility result for the method. [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.08983)
- Universal Transformers reported benefits from dynamic halting on several structured tasks, with a marginal degradation on machine translation. That is more nuanced than “depth is unrelated to difficulty.” [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/1807.03819v3)
- PonderNet reported positive adaptive-computation results and evaluated parity experiments across ten seeds. Its existence does not establish universal robustness, but neither does a blanket “hyperparameter-sensitive failure” characterization. [arxiv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.05407)
- Mixture-of-Depths uses a predictable total budget with context-dependent token allocation. That is a constrained allocation solution, not static computation everywhere; it reported post-training sampling steps upwards of 50% faster in its evaluated setting. [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02258)


The correct comparison is therefore between **different optimization problems and execution semantics**, not between methods presumed to have failed at one common problem.

## 1. The halting problem: learn continuation value, not desired depth


### Formal statement


Let  be the incremental cost of executing the next stage, and let $\mathcal F_l$ contain the information visible to the controller.


Define conditional stopping risk


$$
R_l
=
\mathbb E[\ell(p_l,Y)\mid\mathcal F_l].
$$


For a specified compute price , the finite-horizon optimal-stopping recursion is


$$
V_N=R_N,
$$


$$
V_l
=
\min\left\{
R_l,\;
\lambda c_{l+1}
+
\mathbb E[V_{l+1}\mid\mathcal F_l]
\right\}.
$$


Define the **advantage of continuing**


$$
A_l
=
R_l-
\left(
\lambda c_{l+1}
+
\mathbb E[V_{l+1}\mid\mathcal F_l]
\right).
$$


The optimal decision is:


$$
\text{continue iff }A_l>0.
$$


**This is the correct decision signal.** It requires no externally supplied depth labels. But estimating it reliably requires information about task outcomes and future computation.


### Why prior objectives can fail mechanically


**First, a mixture loss can reward states that are useful only in combination.**


Training may make  and  jointly predictive without making either a good individual exit. A gate optimized through that mixture need not learn stopping value.


**Second, survival weighting creates gradient starvation.**


For the enumerated objective


$$
J=\mathbb E\left[
\sum_l a_l\ell_l+
\lambda\sum_l r_lc_l
\right],
$$


holding the trajectory fixed, differentiation with respect to a nonterminal gate logit  gives


$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_l}
=
\mathbb E\left[
r_lq_l(1-q_l)
\left(
\ell_l-J^{\mathrm{cont}}_{l+1}
\right)
\right],
$$


where  is the conditional future loss plus future compute cost under the current downstream stopping policy.


The desired comparison is present, but multiplied by:
- : little survival means little training signal.
- : a saturated gate is difficult to reverse.


Deep stages can therefore remain bad because they are rarely trained, while the controller exits early because those stages are bad.


**Third, compute penalties only make sense relative to execution.**


A correctly survival-weighted penalty charges stages that would execute. An ungated penalty may charge nonexistent work or have no useful derivative with respect to stopping.

|---|---|---|

| Removing stage supervision: correlation becomes 0.124, accuracy 0.992 | Intermediate predictive competence matters in this setting | External depth labels are necessary |
| Correlations change sign across  | The reported allocation is not robust across these configurations | Increasing  should theoretically increase correlation monotonically |


Intermediate losses $\ell(p_l,y)$ use **task labels**, not depth labels. They are compatible with the stated prohibition on depth supervision.


Also, allocating more compute to “harder” samples is not always optimal: some hard samples are irreducibly ambiguous, while moderately hard samples are highly improvable.


### Solvability conditions


A practical solution needs:
1. **Predictable continuation advantage:** available features must contain information about .
2. **Competent candidate exits:** deeper and shallower predictions must receive enough training.
3. **A specified utility:** a compute budget or acceptable excess risk must be given.
4. **Compatible execution semantics:** the training objective must evaluate the policy actually deployed.


To replace an arbitrary penalty coefficient with an interpretable specification, use


$$
\min_\theta \mathbb E[\ell(p_H,Y)]
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
\mathbb E[C_H]\le B.
$$


A dual variable can adapt to meet . This removes manual selection of the *tradeoff multiplier*, not all optimizer settings or the need to choose .


For scaling, use actual cost


$$
C_l=\sum_{j=1}^{l}c_j,
$$


not depth index alone. If the budget is a fraction of full computation, normalize by . If it is an absolute latency budget, do not.


### Minimal falsifiable test


Across :
- Fix the task distribution, utility specification, and controller-selection rule.
- Train without depth labels or depth-specific retuning.
- On held-out data, execute full trajectories to estimate whether continuation was beneficial.
- Measure decision regret, task risk, and actual compute.


The hypothesis is falsified if the predicted sign of  fails to outperform a constant stopping decision, or if matched-budget performance does not transfer across .


**Primary metric:** regret against a matched-information stopping oracle—not Pearson correlation with difficulty.

## 2. Target-free completion: possible as a conditional guarantee, not a universal invariant


### Formal statement


A useful completion variable should answer something like


$$
P\left(
\text{additional computation improves loss by more than }\epsilon
\mid\mathcal F_l
\right)
\le\delta.
$$


This is stronger and more decision-relevant than asking whether the state has stabilized.


An alternative uses expected improvement under an admissible continuation policy :


$$
G_l^\pi
=
\mathbb E[
\ell(p_l,Y)-\ell(p_{T^\pi},Y)
\mid\mathcal F_l
].
$$


Completion is then relative to a policy class, risk tolerance, and cost—not an intrinsic property of .


### Why the proposed target-aware formula is not sufficient


The issue is **unavailability of **, not . Labels need not be differentiated for  to be evaluated during training.


Additional issues are:
- $\ell_N$ need not be the minimum loss along the trajectory.
-  can be negative if an intermediate loss exceeds the initial loss.
- When $\ell_1\approx\ell_N$, normalization becomes dominated by .
- A sample can improve later even after several stages of apparent stagnation.


It measures progress relative to an endpoint. It does not prove that stopping now is optimal.

2.612,

- Selection advantages from target-aware evaluation, if labels enter the stopping decision.


The last possibility must be explicitly excluded before calling these deployable adaptive-inference gains.


### Solvability conditions


There are three distinct routes.


**A. Learn conditional expected gain using training labels.**


$$
\widehat G_l=f_\phi(\mathcal F_l).
$$


Training targets can be realized loss differences between stopping and specified continuations. At inference, the estimator uses no label.


This is possible without depth labels. It gives statistical predictions, not universal correctness.


**B. Use a certified numerical residual.**


Suppose the same map  is repeatedly applied and is contractive:


$$
\|F(u)-F(v)\|\le\kappa\|u-v\|,
\qquad \kappa<1.
$$


For its fixed point ,


$$
\|h_l-h^\star\|
\le
\frac{\|F(h_l)-h_l\|}{1-\kappa}.
$$


If $g=\operatorname{readout}$ is -Lipschitz, then


$$
\|g(h_l)-g(h^\star)\|
\le
\frac{L_g}{1-\kappa}\|F(h_l)-h_l\|.
$$


This certifies closeness to the converged prediction. It certifies task-loss closeness only with an additional loss regularity bound.


It also requires a valid contraction constant and possibly another evaluation of .


**C. Use task-specific verification.**


Some tasks expose label-free checks: constraint satisfaction, feasibility residuals, consistency conditions, or independently checkable answers. Such checks can supply stronger evidence than generic confidence.


### Strong limitation


A tiny residual does not establish that the limiting prediction is correct.


A system can converge perfectly to the wrong answer:


$$
F(h^\star)=h^\star
\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad
\ell(g(h^\star),y)\text{ is small}.
$$


Likewise, for arbitrary stage-dependent maps, identical prefix states can precede either negligible or substantial future improvement.


### Minimal falsifiable test


Predefine  and . Whenever the label-free estimator declares completion, continue offline and measure actual improvement.


Test whether


$$
P(
\ell(p_l,Y)-\ell(p_{T^\pi},Y)>\epsilon
\mid \text{declared complete}
)
\le\delta
$$


holds on held-out data, including depth and distribution shifts.


Failure of this conditional guarantee falsifies the completion claim. Correlation with difficulty does not test it.

## 3. Soft–hard mismatch: measure per-input execution and decision discrepancy


### Formal statement


Let


$$
H_\tau(x)=\min\{l:q_l(x)\ge\tau\},
$$


with forced exit at .


The soft hazard distribution is


$$
P_s(T=l\mid x)=a_l(x),
$$


while deterministic inference induces


$$
P_h(T=l\mid x)=\mathbf 1[l=H_\tau(x)].
$$


These are different conditional distributions even when their aggregate statistics look similar.


### Why aggregate KL is insufficient


With terminal absorption,


$$
\sum_l a_l(x)=1.
$$


Therefore, a Bernoulli variable parameterized by  carries **no information about exit depth**.


Comparing it with an exit-gate probability is not comparing the soft and hard stopping distributions.


Even a correctly defined KL between dataset-level depth histograms would miss samplewise disagreement. For example:


| Sample | Soft exit mass | Hard exit |
|---|---|---|
| A | All mass at stage 1 | Stage 10 |
| B | All mass at stage 10 | Stage 1 |


Both methods have the same marginal depth histogram and mean depth. Every sample disagrees.


Thus, the reported KL of – does not establish semantic alignment.


### Correct metric hierarchy


**1. Per-input stopping-distribution mismatch**


The total variation distance from the deterministic exit distribution is exactly


$$
D_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(a(x),\delta_{H_\tau(x)}\right)
=
1-a_{H_\tau(x)}(x).
$$


This asks whether the soft distribution actually concentrates on the deployed exit.


**2. Per-input depth mismatch**


$$
W_1\!\left(a(x),\delta_{H_\tau(x)}\right)
=
\sum_l a_l(x)|l-H_\tau(x)|.
$$


Use cost differences instead of stage differences when stages have unequal cost.


**3. Predictive discrepancy**


$$
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(p_s(x),p_h(x)),
$$


alongside classification disagreement or another task-appropriate output metric.


**4. Task and compute discrepancy**


$$
\mathbb E[\ell(p_h,Y)-\ell(p_s,Y)],
$$


$$
\mathbb E[C_{H_\tau}]
-
\mathbb E\left[\sum_l a_lC_l\right].
$$


Report tails and subgroups, not just averages.


### What about trajectory distance?


Under the setup as written, if the gate does not alter state updates, then before hard exit:


$$
h_l^{\mathrm{soft}}=h_l^{\mathrm{hard}}.
$$


There is no prefix-trajectory mismatch to discover. The mismatch is in **which output is returned**.


After exit, the hard trajectory is undefined unless an explicit continuation or absorbing-state convention is introduced.


Trajectory divergence becomes meaningful when gating changes writes, attention, communication, or subsequent state updates.


### Gradient consistency


For a deterministic threshold gate, the selected stage is locally constant away from threshold boundaries. Therefore, the exact gradient of the per-sample hard loss with respect to gate-only parameters is zero almost everywhere, and can be undefined at a switch.


A soft surrogate can provide a useful gradient, but that does not make it the exact hard-execution gradient.


### Solvability conditions


Exact output equivalence requires special structure, such as


$$
\sum_l a_lh_l=h_{H_\tau},
$$


or at least equality after readout.


This holds if:
- The mass is genuinely concentrated at the hard exit.
- All weighted states are equivalent for prediction.
- A special cancellation or affine identity applies.


Finite sigmoid logits do not generically produce one-hot exit distributions.


### Minimal falsifiable test


For every held-out input, record:
-  and .
- .
- Cost-weighted .
- Soft and hard predictions.
- Paired task-loss difference.


A claim of soft–hard equivalence fails if predictive or stopping discrepancies exceed a preregistered tolerance, regardless of aggregate KL.

## 4. Identity collapse: enforce useful computation, not mandatory movement


### Formal statement


The undesirable condition is not


$$
h_{l+1}\approx h_l
$$


by itself. It is that the system pays for stages that do not improve any relevant decision or enable later improvement.


A natural quantity is the expected value of the stage under a specified continuation:


$$
U_l
=
\mathbb E[
\ell(p_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{skip}\ l},Y)
-
\ell(p_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{execute}\ l},Y)
].
$$


This is an intervention-based utility, not a norm of the residual.


### Why the supplied evidence is suggestive but incomplete


The reported cosine is


$$
\cos(h_1,h_{12})=0.9975.
$$


For unit-normalized states, this corresponds to


$$
\left\|
\frac{h_1}{\|h_1\|}
-
\frac{h_{12}}{\|h_{12}\|}
\right\|
=
\sqrt{2-2(0.9975)}
\approx0.0707.
$$


That is small angular movement, but not zero movement. It also says nothing about norms.


More importantly:
- Small state changes can cross a decision boundary.
- Large state changes can lie in a readout nullspace.
- A strong initial representation may legitimately need almost no further computation.
- Terminal changes of – versus mid-stage changes of – are not directly interpretable without normalization conventions and task effects.


The $79\%/21\%/0\%$ exit distribution establishes **early-exit concentration**. It does not, alone, establish incorrect stopping.


### Why movement penalties fail mechanically


Consider an objective that enforces


$$
\|h_{l+1}-h_l\|\ge m.
$$


If the readout is insensitive to a subspace $\mathcal N$, then the model can choose


$$
h_{l+1}=h_l+v_l,
\qquad v_l\in\mathcal N,
$$


and satisfy the movement requirement without changing task behavior.


It can also oscillate between states, increasing path length without improving the answer.


Your proposed residual scaling has an additional problem:


$$
h_{l+1}=h_l+\alpha_lf_l(h_l).
$$


If , finite  cannot create progress. If  is mostly noise, increasing  amplifies noise.


### Solvability conditions


Without changing stage architecture, reasonable objective-level interventions include:
- **Training every candidate exit:** prevent useful intermediate predictors from being starved.
- **Training counterfactual continuations:** evaluate later stages during training even when the current gate prefers to stop.
- **Penalizing unjustified stopping:** use future loss reductions to train a continuation-value predictor.
- **Testing stage deletion:** remove or bypass stages to determine whether apparent inactivity is truly redundant.


These do not require depth labels.


However, a universal guarantee that *every stage* makes meaningful task progress is impossible: some inputs are already solved, and some tasks require several individually uninformative steps before a useful result emerges.


Even a monotonic-loss penalty,


$$
\max(0,\ell_{l+1}-\ell_l),
$$


can penalize necessary intermediate representations. It is a task-dependent inductive bias, not a general remedy.


### Minimal falsifiable test


For low-change stages, compare:
1. Normal execution.
2. Stage bypass.
3. Forced continuation after the proposed early exit.


Measure paired output and loss changes.


The collapse diagnosis gains support if bypassing many stages has negligible effect **and** early stopping misses substantial later gains.


If bypassing the stages degrades performance despite small cosine changes, the geometric diagnosis is falsified.

## 6. Causal evidence: label-free behavioral effects are measurable; task value is not universal


### Formal statement


Distinguish:


$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{pred}}
=
D\left(
p_{\mathrm{out}}^{do(h_l=v)},
p_{\mathrm{out}}
\right)
$$


from


$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{task}}
=
\ell(p_{\mathrm{out}}^{do(h_l=v)},y)
-
\ell(p_{\mathrm{out}},y).
$$


The first measures a change in model behavior. The second measures whether the change helps or hurts the task.


**An intervention can change the prediction substantially and improve, worsen, or preserve task performance.**


### Why controller confidence and geometry do not establish causality


A gate value of  establishes only what the gate predicts.


A cosine of  establishes only a geometric relationship.


Neither tells us:
- Whether early computation was necessary.
- Whether later computation changes the answer.
- Whether the change improves expected loss.


Likewise, weak correlation between an emission gate and a downstream intervention effect means the gate is not a reliable proxy for that chosen effect. It does not identify the reason: the effect metric, intervention, gate objective, or downstream compensation may each matter.


### Assessment of the proposed estimators


| Candidate | Label-free? | What it measures | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deterministic perturbation response | Yes | Finite behavioral sensitivity | Requires extra executions; interventions may be off-distribution |
| Downstream Jacobian magnitude | Yes | Local sensitivity of outputs to state | Not the sign of task benefit; typically requires downstream computation |
| Loss gradient | Usually needs a loss target | Local effect on that loss | Predicted labels or entropy change the target being measured |
| Conditional mutual information | Sometimes estimable under added assumptions | Statistical dependence | Not generally a causal or task-utility measure |
| Calibrated gain predictor | Labels during training only | Conditional expected task benefit | Depends on calibration and distribution stability |


The proposed mutual information has a particularly important degeneracy. If the transition is deterministic,


$$
h_l=F_l(h_{l-1}),
$$


then conditioned on ,  contributes no additional randomness:


$$
I(h_l;h_{\mathrm{out}}\mid h_{l-1})=0
$$


under the standard conditional-information interpretation.


Yet intervening on  can have a large downstream effect. Deterministic mediation is a simple example of why conditional dependence and intervention effects are different.


### Solvability conditions


At inference, without labels, one can measure:
- Prediction changes under specified interventions.
- Constraint violations.
- Sensitivity to deterministic perturbations.
- A learned estimate of expected task effect.


One cannot universally measure the realized task-loss effect without knowing enough about the target or a valid substitute for it.


There is also a compute accounting issue: executing several counterfactual continuations may cost more than simply continuing once.


### Minimal falsifiable test


Use a fixed, declared intervention such as bypassing one residual update. On a held-out labeled set:
- Compute the label-free proxy.
- Measure the actual intervention-induced loss change.
- Test sign prediction, calibration, and whether the resulting decisions improve risk at matched total compute.


A proxy is rejected as a stopping signal if it predicts behavioral sensitivity but not beneficial continuation.


Labels belong in this validation experiment; they need not be available to the deployed controller.

\Rightarrow

\Rightarrow

\Rightarrow
\text{poor early exits}
\Rightarrow

$$

$$
\text{continue when conditional benefit exceeds conditional cost}.
$$


There need not be an invariant parameter value, gate temperature, or depth–difficulty correlation.

- Common units for task benefit and compute.
- Fixed budget or excess-risk specifications.
- Sufficient training of all candidate exits.
- Continuation targets defined against a specified policy.
- Calibration checks separated from representation-quality checks.


A meta-optimizer can help within a task family. It cannot provide distribution-free guarantees or remove the need to specify utility.


Ensembling can reduce estimator variance, but:
- Its overhead must be charged.
- Shared systematic errors remain.
- It does not repair soft–hard semantics.


### Minimal falsifiable test


To test chaos rather than generic sensitivity:
1. Fix task data, data order, optimizer, all hyperparameters, and numerical settings.
2. Perturb only the initial parameters by a small controlled amount.
3. Track divergence under the same update map.
4. Test whether a positive finite-time divergence rate persists across perturbation sizes and horizons.


An initial transient of exponential separation alone is not sufficient evidence of a chaotic attractor.


To test the more actionable hypothesis, freeze the backbone and train the controller against fixed continuation outcomes. If instability largely disappears, co-adaptation—not an intrinsic inability to learn stopping—is implicated.

## Cross-cutting synthesis


### 1. Common root: not seven independent failures


The problems fall into three interacting groups.


| Root issue | Problems most directly affected | Missing object |
|---|---|---|
| Decision semantics | 1, 3, 7 | The deployed stopping policy and its actual objective |
| Observability of useful progress | 2, 4, 6 | Conditional task benefit of future computation |
| Optimization and calibration | 1, 4, 5, 7 | Comparable evidence and non-starved learning signals |


The deepest common error is:


$$
\boxed{
\text{A proxy for computation is treated as a certificate of useful computation.}
}
$$


Confidence is not correctness. Movement is not progress. Sensitivity is not utility. Difficulty is not improvability. A normalized gate is not a calibrated decision.


These distinctions explain much of the evidence without invoking a mysterious universal failure mode.


### 2. Unifying principle: separate prediction, continuation value, and execution


The most promising principle is **decision-aligned adaptive computation**:


1. **Prediction:** each candidate exit produces a task prediction.
2. **Valuation:** estimate the conditional improvement available through continuation.
3. **Execution:** compare that improvement with an explicit cost or risk tolerance.
4. **Verification:** evaluate the same execution policy that will be deployed.


This is not necessarily a new stage architecture. Much of it is an objective and measurement discipline.


It addresses several problems simultaneously:
- Halting learns a decision-relevant quantity.
- Target-free inference uses a learned conditional expectation.
- Stage utility is evaluated in task units rather than geometric units.
- Competing signals have commensurate meanings.
- Scaling is expressed through actual compute cost.


**But it does not erase the differentiable-training versus hard-threshold conflict.** One must explicitly choose among:
- Preserving soft execution at inference.
- Accepting a surrogate for hard execution and bounding its deployment gap.
- Using a different deterministic, piecewise-differentiable execution construction.
- Restricting the model class so soft and hard outputs become equivalent.


There is no generic normalization trick that avoids this choice.


### 3. Strongest negative results


#### A. Generic soft mixtures cannot equal hard first-crossing execution


Fix two distinct states  and force exit at stage two.


The soft output is


$$
S(q)=qh_1+(1-q)h_2,
$$


while the hard output is


$$
H(q)=
\begin{cases}
h_2,&q<\tau,\\
h_1,&q\ge\tau.
\end{cases}
$$


 is continuous and generally lies between the states.  switches discontinuously.


They cannot agree for all .


This already disproves generic exact train–inference output equivalence for the stated setup.


#### B. Exact hard gate gradients provide no ordinary per-sample learning signal away from boundaries


With a fixed backbone, changing a gate-only parameter slightly does not change the selected exit unless a threshold is crossed.


Thus the exact per-sample hard loss is piecewise constant in that parameter.


An expected loss over a continuous input distribution can sometimes have a nonzero derivative through moving decision boundaries. But ordinary backpropagation through individual hard decisions does not automatically compute that boundary contribution.


Therefore:


$$
\boxed{
\text{Exact hard thresholding + ordinary pathwise gradients}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{a useful exact gate gradient}.
}
$$


This is narrower and more accurate than saying all hard adaptive computation is nondifferentiable everywhere.


#### C. No universal label-free task-progress invariant exists


Consider two tasks with identical available states and predictions, but different conditional label distributions.


Let the current and future predictions favor different labels. Continuing helps under one task distribution and hurts under the other.


Any target-free rule receiving the same information makes the same decision in both worlds. It must be wrong in at least one.


Learning a known task distribution can resolve this ambiguity statistically. A universal state-only invariant cannot.


#### D. No universal hyperparameter-free accuracy–compute optimum exists


Suppose continuation reduces expected loss by  and costs .


It is optimal under the penalized objective exactly when


$$
g>\lambda c.
$$


Different users or applications can legitimately choose different . A budget or risk tolerance expresses the same preference in another form.


No algorithm can infer a unique correct preference from the state trajectory alone.


### 4. What single experiment could falsify the entire paradigm?


**None.** Adaptive computation is a broad class of strategies, not one empirical hypothesis.


There are simple task distributions where some inputs require one operation and others require many; an observable input property identifies which. Adaptation can then strictly improve expected compute at fixed accuracy.


What can be falsified is a claim such as:


> “This model family benefits from input-dependent stopping on this task distribution at this compute scale.”


The strongest compact test is a **matched-budget oracle-gap experiment**.


For full held-out trajectories, estimate:
- : the best input-independent depth policy, including mixtures of fixed depths, at budget .
- : the best stopping policy restricted to information actually available at inference.
- : the learned controller.


Then decompose


$$
R_{\mathrm{learned}}(B)-R_{\mathrm{fixed}}(B)
=
\underbrace{
R_{\mathrm{info}}^\star(B)-R_{\mathrm{fixed}}(B)
}_{\text{available adaptation benefit}}
+
\underbrace{
R_{\mathrm{learned}}(B)-R_{\mathrm{info}}^\star(B)
}_{\text{controller regret}}.
$$


Interpretation:
- No oracle advantage: little stopping opportunity exists in those trajectories under the allowed information.
- Oracle advantage, learned controller failure: estimation or optimization is the bottleneck.
- Advantage disappears after controller overhead: the computational benefit is not operationally useful.
- Advantage exists only for a label-aware hindsight oracle: useful exit variation exists, but may not be observable at deployment.


For a globally trained model-family claim, matched-budget fixed-depth models must also be trained separately. A frozen-backbone oracle test diagnoses one representation, not every possible representation.


### 5. Five-year bet


I would fund **calibrated continuation-value prediction, paired with task-specific certificates where available**.


The program would have three priorities:


| Priority | Why fund it | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Predict future task-loss improvement from current information | Directly targets the stopping decision | Miscalibration under distribution shift |
| Develop verifiable residuals or task certificates | Can provide stronger stopping guarantees | Limited task coverage |
| Evaluate adaptation using oracle gaps and full compute accounting | Separates representation, observability, and controller failures | Expensive evaluation |


I would not make the primary bet on:
- Larger collections of mutually competing gates.
- A universal state-motion measure of completion.
- Temperature schedules chosen from increasingly large sweeps.
- Maximizing difficulty–depth correlation.
- Claiming alignment from aggregate KL.


### Final assessment


The reported results are consistent with **misaligned objectives, weakly identifiable stopping signals, and self-reinforcing optimization failures**. They do not establish chaos, and they do not falsify adaptive computation.


The central scientific target should be:


$$
\boxed{
\text{Predictable, calibrated marginal task benefit per unit of additional compute.}
}
$$


That quantity is learnable under assumptions, certifiable in restricted settings, and impossible to recover universally from label-free geometry alone.


**Suggested next step:** run the matched-budget oracle-gap experiment before another hyperparameter sweep; it determines whether the missing ingredient is useful deeper computation, observable stopping information, or a controller capable of exploiting it.


## Executive conclusion


**The central missing quantity is not “completion,” state movement, or difficulty. It is the conditional value of additional computation.**


For an information set $\mathcal F_l$ available at stage , the relevant question is:


$$
\text{How much expected task loss can continuing reduce, relative to its cost?}
$$


Most of the reported failures arise from substituting something easier to measure—confidence, residual size, a bounded resistance signal, or a soft halting distribution—for that quantity.


However, **there is no general solution satisfying all the stated requirements exactly**. Three obstacles must be separated:


1. **Semantic incompatibility:** a weighted mixture of intermediate states is generally not the same computation as deterministic first-crossing inference.
2. **Non-identifiability:** local, label-free state geometry cannot universally determine whether additional computation will improve task performance.
3. **Unavoidable preferences:** choosing an accuracy–compute tradeoff requires a budget, risk tolerance, or utility specification. Normalization cannot eliminate that choice.


These are structural issues, not evidence that adaptive computation itself is futile.


### Evidence qualification


I treat the supplied numerical results as **reported experimental observations**, not independently verified measurements. Without sample counts, uncertainty estimates, metric definitions, and matched controls, they support diagnoses and testable hypotheses—not universal conclusions.


The historical failure table also needs qualification:
- ACT explicitly proposed deterministic, differentiable adaptive computation; particular instability observations are not an impossibility result for the method. [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.08983)
- Universal Transformers reported benefits from dynamic halting on several structured tasks, with a marginal degradation on machine translation. That is more nuanced than “depth is unrelated to difficulty.” [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/1807.03819v3)
- PonderNet reported positive adaptive-computation results and evaluated parity experiments across ten seeds. Its existence does not establish universal robustness, but neither does a blanket “hyperparameter-sensitive failure” characterization. [arxiv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.05407)
- Mixture-of-Depths uses a predictable total budget with context-dependent token allocation. That is a constrained allocation solution, not static computation everywhere; it reported post-training sampling steps upwards of 50% faster in its evaluated setting. [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02258)


The correct comparison is therefore between **different optimization problems and execution semantics**, not between methods presumed to have failed at one common problem.

## 1. The halting problem: learn continuation value, not desired depth


### Formal statement


Let  be the incremental cost of executing the next stage, and let $\mathcal F_l$ contain the information visible to the controller.


Define conditional stopping risk


$$
R_l
=
\mathbb E[\ell(p_l,Y)\mid\mathcal F_l].
$$


For a specified compute price , the finite-horizon optimal-stopping recursion is


$$
V_N=R_N,
$$


$$
V_l
=
\min\left\{
R_l,\;
\lambda c_{l+1}
+
\mathbb E[V_{l+1}\mid\mathcal F_l]
\right\}.
$$


Define the **advantage of continuing**


$$
A_l
=
R_l-
\left(
\lambda c_{l+1}
+
\mathbb E[V_{l+1}\mid\mathcal F_l]
\right).
$$


The optimal decision is:


$$
\text{continue iff }A_l>0.
$$


**This is the correct decision signal.** It requires no externally supplied depth labels. But estimating it reliably requires information about task outcomes and future computation.


### Why prior objectives can fail mechanically


**First, a mixture loss can reward states that are useful only in combination.**


Training may make  and  jointly predictive without making either a good individual exit. A gate optimized through that mixture need not learn stopping value.


**Second, survival weighting creates gradient starvation.**


For the enumerated objective


$$
J=\mathbb E\left[
\sum_l a_l\ell_l+
\lambda\sum_l r_lc_l
\right],
$$


holding the trajectory fixed, differentiation with respect to a nonterminal gate logit  gives


$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_l}
=
\mathbb E\left[
r_lq_l(1-q_l)
\left(
\ell_l-J^{\mathrm{cont}}_{l+1}
\right)
\right],
$$


where  is the conditional future loss plus future compute cost under the current downstream stopping policy.


The desired comparison is present, but multiplied by:
- : little survival means little training signal.
- : a saturated gate is difficult to reverse.


Deep stages can therefore remain bad because they are rarely trained, while the controller exits early because those stages are bad.


**Third, compute penalties only make sense relative to execution.**


A correctly survival-weighted penalty charges stages that would execute. An ungated penalty may charge nonexistent work or have no useful derivative with respect to stopping.


### What the supplied numbers establish


| Observation | Supported interpretation | Not established |
|---|---|---|
| Removing cost: accuracy remains 1.000, correlation falls from 0.771 to 0.103 | Task accuracy alone does not identify the desired allocation | Positive difficulty correlation is necessary for useful adaptation |
| Removing gated cost: accuracy falls to 0.695 | This objective change strongly damages the reported system | Gating is universally necessary in that exact form |
| Removing stage supervision: correlation becomes 0.124, accuracy 0.992 | Intermediate predictive competence matters in this setting | External depth labels are necessary |
| Correlations change sign across  | The reported allocation is not robust across these configurations | Increasing  should theoretically increase correlation monotonically |


Intermediate losses $\ell(p_l,y)$ use **task labels**, not depth labels. They are compatible with the stated prohibition on depth supervision.


Also, allocating more compute to “harder” samples is not always optimal: some hard samples are irreducibly ambiguous, while moderately hard samples are highly improvable.


### Solvability conditions


A practical solution needs:
1. **Predictable continuation advantage:** available features must contain information about .
2. **Competent candidate exits:** deeper and shallower predictions must receive enough training.
3. **A specified utility:** a compute budget or acceptable excess risk must be given.
4. **Compatible execution semantics:** the training objective must evaluate the policy actually deployed.


To replace an arbitrary penalty coefficient with an interpretable specification, use


$$
\min_\theta \mathbb E[\ell(p_H,Y)]
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
\mathbb E[C_H]\le B.
$$


A dual variable can adapt to meet . This removes manual selection of the *tradeoff multiplier*, not all optimizer settings or the need to choose .


For scaling, use actual cost


$$
C_l=\sum_{j=1}^{l}c_j,
$$


not depth index alone. If the budget is a fraction of full computation, normalize by . If it is an absolute latency budget, do not.


### Minimal falsifiable test


Across :
- Fix the task distribution, utility specification, and controller-selection rule.
- Train without depth labels or depth-specific retuning.
- On held-out data, execute full trajectories to estimate whether continuation was beneficial.
- Measure decision regret, task risk, and actual compute.


The hypothesis is falsified if the predicted sign of  fails to outperform a constant stopping decision, or if matched-budget performance does not transfer across .


**Primary metric:** regret against a matched-information stopping oracle—not Pearson correlation with difficulty.

## 2. Target-free completion: possible as a conditional guarantee, not a universal invariant


### Formal statement


A useful completion variable should answer something like


$$
P\left(
\text{additional computation improves loss by more than }\epsilon
\mid\mathcal F_l
\right)
\le\delta.
$$


This is stronger and more decision-relevant than asking whether the state has stabilized.


An alternative uses expected improvement under an admissible continuation policy :


$$
G_l^\pi
=
\mathbb E[
\ell(p_l,Y)-\ell(p_{T^\pi},Y)
\mid\mathcal F_l
].
$$


Completion is then relative to a policy class, risk tolerance, and cost—not an intrinsic property of .


### Why the proposed target-aware formula is not sufficient


The issue is **unavailability of **, not . Labels need not be differentiated for  to be evaluated during training.


Additional issues are:
- $\ell_N$ need not be the minimum loss along the trajectory.
-  can be negative if an intermediate loss exceeds the initial loss.
- When $\ell_1\approx\ell_N$, normalization becomes dominated by .
- A sample can improve later even after several stages of apparent stagnation.


It measures progress relative to an endpoint. It does not prove that stopping now is optimal.

The last possibility must be explicitly excluded before calling these deployable adaptive-inference gains.


### Solvability conditions


There are three distinct routes.


**A. Learn conditional expected gain using training labels.**


$$
\widehat G_l=f_\phi(\mathcal F_l).
$$


Training targets can be realized loss differences between stopping and specified continuations. At inference, the estimator uses no label.


This is possible without depth labels. It gives statistical predictions, not universal correctness.


**B. Use a certified numerical residual.**


Suppose the same map  is repeatedly applied and is contractive:


$$
\|F(u)-F(v)\|\le\kappa\|u-v\|,
\qquad \kappa<1.
$$


For its fixed point ,


$$
\|h_l-h^\star\|
\le
\frac{\|F(h_l)-h_l\|}{1-\kappa}.
$$


If $g=\operatorname{readout}$ is -Lipschitz, then


$$
\|g(h_l)-g(h^\star)\|
\le
\frac{L_g}{1-\kappa}\|F(h_l)-h_l\|.
$$


This certifies closeness to the converged prediction. It certifies task-loss closeness only with an additional loss regularity bound.


It also requires a valid contraction constant and possibly another evaluation of .


**C. Use task-specific verification.**


Some tasks expose label-free checks: constraint satisfaction, feasibility residuals, consistency conditions, or independently checkable answers. Such checks can supply stronger evidence than generic confidence.


### Strong limitation


A tiny residual does not establish that the limiting prediction is correct.


A system can converge perfectly to the wrong answer:


$$
F(h^\star)=h^\star
\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad
\ell(g(h^\star),y)\text{ is small}.
$$


Likewise, for arbitrary stage-dependent maps, identical prefix states can precede either negligible or substantial future improvement.


### Minimal falsifiable test


Predefine  and . Whenever the label-free estimator declares completion, continue offline and measure actual improvement.


Test whether


$$
P(
\ell(p_l,Y)-\ell(p_{T^\pi},Y)>\epsilon
\mid \text{declared complete}
)
\le\delta
$$


holds on held-out data, including depth and distribution shifts.


Failure of this conditional guarantee falsifies the completion claim. Correlation with difficulty does not test it.

## 3. Soft–hard mismatch: measure per-input execution and decision discrepancy


### Formal statement


Let


$$
H_\tau(x)=\min\{l:q_l(x)\ge\tau\},
$$


with forced exit at .


The soft hazard distribution is


$$
P_s(T=l\mid x)=a_l(x),
$$


while deterministic inference induces


$$
P_h(T=l\mid x)=\mathbf 1[l=H_\tau(x)].
$$


These are different conditional distributions even when their aggregate statistics look similar.


### Why aggregate KL is insufficient


With terminal absorption,


$$
\sum_l a_l(x)=1.
$$


Therefore, a Bernoulli variable parameterized by  carries **no information about exit depth**.


Comparing it with an exit-gate probability is not comparing the soft and hard stopping distributions.


Even a correctly defined KL between dataset-level depth histograms would miss samplewise disagreement. For example:


| Sample | Soft exit mass | Hard exit |
|---|---|---|
| A | All mass at stage 1 | Stage 10 |
| B | All mass at stage 10 | Stage 1 |


Both methods have the same marginal depth histogram and mean depth. Every sample disagrees.


Thus, the reported KL of – does not establish semantic alignment.


### Correct metric hierarchy


**1. Per-input stopping-distribution mismatch**


The total variation distance from the deterministic exit distribution is exactly


$$
D_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(a(x),\delta_{H_\tau(x)}\right)
=
1-a_{H_\tau(x)}(x).
$$


This asks whether the soft distribution actually concentrates on the deployed exit.


**2. Per-input depth mismatch**


$$
W_1\!\left(a(x),\delta_{H_\tau(x)}\right)
=
\sum_l a_l(x)|l-H_\tau(x)|.
$$


Use cost differences instead of stage differences when stages have unequal cost.


**3. Predictive discrepancy**


$$
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(p_s(x),p_h(x)),
$$


alongside classification disagreement or another task-appropriate output metric.


**4. Task and compute discrepancy**


$$
\mathbb E[\ell(p_h,Y)-\ell(p_s,Y)],
$$


$$
\mathbb E[C_{H_\tau}]
-
\mathbb E\left[\sum_l a_lC_l\right].
$$


Report tails and subgroups, not just averages.


### What about trajectory distance?


Under the setup as written, if the gate does not alter state updates, then before hard exit:


$$
h_l^{\mathrm{soft}}=h_l^{\mathrm{hard}}.
$$


There is no prefix-trajectory mismatch to discover. The mismatch is in **which output is returned**.


After exit, the hard trajectory is undefined unless an explicit continuation or absorbing-state convention is introduced.


Trajectory divergence becomes meaningful when gating changes writes, attention, communication, or subsequent state updates.


### Gradient consistency


For a deterministic threshold gate, the selected stage is locally constant away from threshold boundaries. Therefore, the exact gradient of the per-sample hard loss with respect to gate-only parameters is zero almost everywhere, and can be undefined at a switch.


A soft surrogate can provide a useful gradient, but that does not make it the exact hard-execution gradient.


### Solvability conditions


Exact output equivalence requires special structure, such as


$$
\sum_l a_lh_l=h_{H_\tau},
$$


or at least equality after readout.


This holds if:
- The mass is genuinely concentrated at the hard exit.
- All weighted states are equivalent for prediction.
- A special cancellation or affine identity applies.


Finite sigmoid logits do not generically produce one-hot exit distributions.


### Minimal falsifiable test


For every held-out input, record:
-  and .
- .
- Cost-weighted .
- Soft and hard predictions.
- Paired task-loss difference.


A claim of soft–hard equivalence fails if predictive or stopping discrepancies exceed a preregistered tolerance, regardless of aggregate KL.

## 4. Identity collapse: enforce useful computation, not mandatory movement


### Formal statement


The undesirable condition is not


$$
h_{l+1}\approx h_l
$$


by itself. It is that the system pays for stages that do not improve any relevant decision or enable later improvement.


A natural quantity is the expected value of the stage under a specified continuation:


$$
U_l
=
\mathbb E[
\ell(p_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{skip}\ l},Y)
-
\ell(p_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{execute}\ l},Y)
].
$$


This is an intervention-based utility, not a norm of the residual.


### Why the supplied evidence is suggestive but incomplete


The reported cosine is


$$
\cos(h_1,h_{12})=0.9975.
$$


For unit-normalized states, this corresponds to


$$
\left\|
\frac{h_1}{\|h_1\|}
-
\frac{h_{12}}{\|h_{12}\|}
\right\|
=
\sqrt{2-2(0.9975)}
\approx0.0707.
$$


That is small angular movement, but not zero movement. It also says nothing about norms.


More importantly:
- Small state changes can cross a decision boundary.
- Large state changes can lie in a readout nullspace.
- A strong initial representation may legitimately need almost no further computation.
- Terminal changes of – versus mid-stage changes of – are not directly interpretable without normalization conventions and task effects.


The $79\%/21\%/0\%$ exit distribution establishes **early-exit concentration**. It does not, alone, establish incorrect stopping.


### Why movement penalties fail mechanically


Consider an objective that enforces


$$
\|h_{l+1}-h_l\|\ge m.
$$


If the readout is insensitive to a subspace $\mathcal N$, then the model can choose


$$
h_{l+1}=h_l+v_l,
\qquad v_l\in\mathcal N,
$$


and satisfy the movement requirement without changing task behavior.


It can also oscillate between states, increasing path length without improving the answer.


Your proposed residual scaling has an additional problem:


$$
h_{l+1}=h_l+\alpha_lf_l(h_l).
$$


If , finite  cannot create progress. If  is mostly noise, increasing  amplifies noise.


### Solvability conditions


Without changing stage architecture, reasonable objective-level interventions include:
- **Training every candidate exit:** prevent useful intermediate predictors from being starved.
- **Training counterfactual continuations:** evaluate later stages during training even when the current gate prefers to stop.
- **Penalizing unjustified stopping:** use future loss reductions to train a continuation-value predictor.
- **Testing stage deletion:** remove or bypass stages to determine whether apparent inactivity is truly redundant.


These do not require depth labels.


However, a universal guarantee that *every stage* makes meaningful task progress is impossible: some inputs are already solved, and some tasks require several individually uninformative steps before a useful result emerges.


Even a monotonic-loss penalty,


$$
\max(0,\ell_{l+1}-\ell_l),
$$


can penalize necessary intermediate representations. It is a task-dependent inductive bias, not a general remedy.


### Minimal falsifiable test


For low-change stages, compare:
1. Normal execution.
2. Stage bypass.
3. Forced continuation after the proposed early exit.


Measure paired output and loss changes.


The collapse diagnosis gains support if bypassing many stages has negligible effect **and** early stopping misses substantial later gains.


If bypassing the stages degrades performance despite small cosine changes, the geometric diagnosis is falsified.

Moreover, because  can be negative, the purported “resistance” branch can increase the stop logit. Its semantic role is not enforced by its name.


### A genuine scale-invariant construction


For two linear evidence branches, cosine scores give


$$
s_a=
\frac{w_a^\top u_a}{\|w_a\|\|u_a\|},
\qquad
s_b=
\frac{w_b^\top u_b}{\|w_b\|\|u_b\|},
$$


$$
z=\kappa(s_a-s_b).
$$


Then


$$
s_a,s_b\in[-1,1],
\qquad
z\in[-2\kappa,2\kappa].
$$


Each score is invariant to positive rescaling of its weight or feature vector.


But:
- Zero norms require handling.
- An  stabilizer weakens exact invariance near zero.
-  still controls probability sharpness.
- Angular alignment can still make one branch dominate semantically.


There is a useful mathematical limit here:


> A nonconstant function cannot be both continuous at the origin and exactly invariant to all positive input scalings.


Indeed, if  for every , continuity as  implies


$$
f(x)=f(0)
$$


for every .


Exact scale invariance necessarily excludes the origin, introduces a singularity, or relaxes continuity.


### Solvability conditions


The deeper solution is to make both terms commensurate:


$$
\underbrace{\text{expected loss reduction}}_{\text{benefit}}
-
\underbrace{\lambda\times\text{additional compute}}_{\text{cost in loss units}}.
$$


If both branches represent calibrated risks or utilities in the same units, their competition has a defined meaning.


Bounding unrelated latent quantities merely makes their competition numerically tidy.


### Minimal falsifiable test


Perform a **function-preserving reparameterization test**:
- Multiply a branch weight by factors spanning several orders of magnitude.
- Apply any compensating transformation needed to preserve the represented evidence.
- Check whether decisions change.


A claimed scale-invariant controller should preserve its decisions under the relevant transformations.


Separately, test whether each branch predicts its stated target. Invariance without calibration does not solve stopping.

## 6. Causal evidence: label-free behavioral effects are measurable; task value is not universal


### Formal statement


Distinguish:


$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{pred}}
=
D\left(
p_{\mathrm{out}}^{do(h_l=v)},
p_{\mathrm{out}}
\right)
$$


from


$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{task}}
=
\ell(p_{\mathrm{out}}^{do(h_l=v)},y)
-
\ell(p_{\mathrm{out}},y).
$$


The first measures a change in model behavior. The second measures whether the change helps or hurts the task.


**An intervention can change the prediction substantially and improve, worsen, or preserve task performance.**


### Why controller confidence and geometry do not establish causality


A gate value of  establishes only what the gate predicts.


A cosine of  establishes only a geometric relationship.


Neither tells us:
- Whether early computation was necessary.
- Whether later computation changes the answer.
- Whether the change improves expected loss.


Likewise, weak correlation between an emission gate and a downstream intervention effect means the gate is not a reliable proxy for that chosen effect. It does not identify the reason: the effect metric, intervention, gate objective, or downstream compensation may each matter.


### Assessment of the proposed estimators


| Candidate | Label-free? | What it measures | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deterministic perturbation response | Yes | Finite behavioral sensitivity | Requires extra executions; interventions may be off-distribution |
| Downstream Jacobian magnitude | Yes | Local sensitivity of outputs to state | Not the sign of task benefit; typically requires downstream computation |
| Loss gradient | Usually needs a loss target | Local effect on that loss | Predicted labels or entropy change the target being measured |
| Conditional mutual information | Sometimes estimable under added assumptions | Statistical dependence | Not generally a causal or task-utility measure |
| Calibrated gain predictor | Labels during training only | Conditional expected task benefit | Depends on calibration and distribution stability |


The proposed mutual information has a particularly important degeneracy. If the transition is deterministic,


$$
h_l=F_l(h_{l-1}),
$$


then conditioned on ,  contributes no additional randomness:


$$
I(h_l;h_{\mathrm{out}}\mid h_{l-1})=0
$$


under the standard conditional-information interpretation.


Yet intervening on  can have a large downstream effect. Deterministic mediation is a simple example of why conditional dependence and intervention effects are different.


### Solvability conditions


At inference, without labels, one can measure:
- Prediction changes under specified interventions.
- Constraint violations.
- Sensitivity to deterministic perturbations.
- A learned estimate of expected task effect.


One cannot universally measure the realized task-loss effect without knowing enough about the target or a valid substitute for it.


There is also a compute accounting issue: executing several counterfactual continuations may cost more than simply continuing once.


### Minimal falsifiable test


Use a fixed, declared intervention such as bypassing one residual update. On a held-out labeled set:
- Compute the label-free proxy.
- Measure the actual intervention-induced loss change.
- Test sign prediction, calibration, and whether the resulting decisions improve risk at matched total compute.


A proxy is rejected as a stopping signal if it predicts behavioral sensitivity but not beneficial continuation.


Labels belong in this validation experiment; they need not be available to the deployed controller.

## 7. The “three-body problem”: coupling is demonstrated; chaos is not


### Formal statement


Let  denote model parameters and  controller parameters. With fixed training protocol, represent training as


$$
(\theta_{t+1},\phi_{t+1})
=
F(\theta_t,\phi_t).
$$


Deterministic chaos concerns sensitive dependence under the **same map** , for example a positive largest Lyapunov exponent along a bounded trajectory.


Changing noise level, loss weight, depth, or task changes  itself.


Therefore, differing outcomes across hyperparameter sweeps do not establish chaos.


### What the reported numbers show


| Reported change | Observation | Defensible conclusion |
|---|---|---|
|  |  | Non-monotonic response in that metric |
| ,  | Correlation  | Poor transfer of this setting |
| Scaling  with  at  | Correlation becomes  | This rescaling improves the reported correlation |


Without a definition of , its changes have no further theoretical interpretation.


These observations are consistent with:
- Nonconvexity and different basins.
- Saturated gate gradients.
- Changing gradient magnitudes across depth.
- Threshold discontinuities in evaluation.
- A compute objective whose units change with .
- Co-adaptation between stage competence and stopping.


None requires chaos.


### The principal feedback loop


A plausible loop is:


$$
\text{early stopping}
\Rightarrow
\text{weak deep-stage gradients}
\Rightarrow
\text{poor continuation value}
\Rightarrow
\text{more early stopping}.
$$


Another is:


$$
\text{strong endpoint-only supervision}
\Rightarrow
\text{late useful transformations}
\Rightarrow
\text{poor early exits}
\Rightarrow
\text{dependence on deep execution}.
$$


The objective, model, and controller jointly shape the very quantity the controller is trying to predict.


### Solvability conditions


An invariant exists at the level of the **decision problem**:


$$
\text{continue when conditional benefit exceeds conditional cost}.
$$


There need not be an invariant parameter value, gate temperature, or depth–difficulty correlation.


Stability can improve through:
- Common units for task benefit and compute.
- Fixed budget or excess-risk specifications.
- Sufficient training of all candidate exits.
- Continuation targets defined against a specified policy.
- Calibration checks separated from representation-quality checks.


A meta-optimizer can help within a task family. It cannot provide distribution-free guarantees or remove the need to specify utility.


Ensembling can reduce estimator variance, but:
- Its overhead must be charged.
- Shared systematic errors remain.
- It does not repair soft–hard semantics.


### Minimal falsifiable test


To test chaos rather than generic sensitivity:
1. Fix task data, data order, optimizer, all hyperparameters, and numerical settings.
2. Perturb only the initial parameters by a small controlled amount.
3. Track divergence under the same update map.
4. Test whether a positive finite-time divergence rate persists across perturbation sizes and horizons.


An initial transient of exponential separation alone is not sufficient evidence of a chaotic attractor.


To test the more actionable hypothesis, freeze the backbone and train the controller against fixed continuation outcomes. If instability largely disappears, co-adaptation—not an intrinsic inability to learn stopping—is implicated.

## Cross-cutting synthesis


### 1. Common root: not seven independent failures


The problems fall into three interacting groups.


| Root issue | Problems most directly affected | Missing object |
|---|---|---|
| Decision semantics | 1, 3, 7 | The deployed stopping policy and its actual objective |
| Observability of useful progress | 2, 4, 6 | Conditional task benefit of future computation |
| Optimization and calibration | 1, 4, 5, 7 | Comparable evidence and non-starved learning signals |


The deepest common error is:


$$
\boxed{
\text{A proxy for computation is treated as a certificate of useful computation.}
}
$$


Confidence is not correctness. Movement is not progress. Sensitivity is not utility. Difficulty is not improvability. A normalized gate is not a calibrated decision.


These distinctions explain much of the evidence without invoking a mysterious universal failure mode.


### 2. Unifying principle: separate prediction, continuation value, and execution


The most promising principle is **decision-aligned adaptive computation**:


1. **Prediction:** each candidate exit produces a task prediction.
2. **Valuation:** estimate the conditional improvement available through continuation.
3. **Execution:** compare that improvement with an explicit cost or risk tolerance.
4. **Verification:** evaluate the same execution policy that will be deployed.


This is not necessarily a new stage architecture. Much of it is an objective and measurement discipline.


It addresses several problems simultaneously:
- Halting learns a decision-relevant quantity.
- Target-free inference uses a learned conditional expectation.
- Stage utility is evaluated in task units rather than geometric units.
- Competing signals have commensurate meanings.
- Scaling is expressed through actual compute cost.


**But it does not erase the differentiable-training versus hard-threshold conflict.** One must explicitly choose among:
- Preserving soft execution at inference.
- Accepting a surrogate for hard execution and bounding its deployment gap.
- Using a different deterministic, piecewise-differentiable execution construction.
- Restricting the model class so soft and hard outputs become equivalent.


There is no generic normalization trick that avoids this choice.


### 3. Strongest negative results


#### A. Generic soft mixtures cannot equal hard first-crossing execution


Fix two distinct states  and force exit at stage two.


The soft output is


$$
S(q)=qh_1+(1-q)h_2,
$$


while the hard output is


$$
H(q)=
\begin{cases}
h_2,&q<\tau,\\
h_1,&q\ge\tau.
\end{cases}
$$


 is continuous and generally lies between the states.  switches discontinuously.


They cannot agree for all .


This already disproves generic exact train–inference output equivalence for the stated setup.


#### B. Exact hard gate gradients provide no ordinary per-sample learning signal away from boundaries


With a fixed backbone, changing a gate-only parameter slightly does not change the selected exit unless a threshold is crossed.


Thus the exact per-sample hard loss is piecewise constant in that parameter.


An expected loss over a continuous input distribution can sometimes have a nonzero derivative through moving decision boundaries. But ordinary backpropagation through individual hard decisions does not automatically compute that boundary contribution.


Therefore:


$$
\boxed{
\text{Exact hard thresholding + ordinary pathwise gradients}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{a useful exact gate gradient}.
}
$$


This is narrower and more accurate than saying all hard adaptive computation is nondifferentiable everywhere.


#### C. No universal label-free task-progress invariant exists


Consider two tasks with identical available states and predictions, but different conditional label distributions.


Let the current and future predictions favor different labels. Continuing helps under one task distribution and hurts under the other.


Any target-free rule receiving the same information makes the same decision in both worlds. It must be wrong in at least one.


Learning a known task distribution can resolve this ambiguity statistically. A universal state-only invariant cannot.


#### D. No universal hyperparameter-free accuracy–compute optimum exists


Suppose continuation reduces expected loss by  and costs .


It is optimal under the penalized objective exactly when


$$
g>\lambda c.
$$


Different users or applications can legitimately choose different . A budget or risk tolerance expresses the same preference in another form.


No algorithm can infer a unique correct preference from the state trajectory alone.


### 4. What single experiment could falsify the entire paradigm?


**None.** Adaptive computation is a broad class of strategies, not one empirical hypothesis.


There are simple task distributions where some inputs require one operation and others require many; an observable input property identifies which. Adaptation can then strictly improve expected compute at fixed accuracy.


What can be falsified is a claim such as:


> “This model family benefits from input-dependent stopping on this task distribution at this compute scale.”


The strongest compact test is a **matched-budget oracle-gap experiment**.


For full held-out trajectories, estimate:
- : the best input-independent depth policy, including mixtures of fixed depths, at budget .
- : the best stopping policy restricted to information actually available at inference.
- : the learned controller.


Then decompose


$$
R_{\mathrm{learned}}(B)-R_{\mathrm{fixed}}(B)
=
\underbrace{
R_{\mathrm{info}}^\star(B)-R_{\mathrm{fixed}}(B)
}_{\text{available adaptation benefit}}
+
\underbrace{
R_{\mathrm{learned}}(B)-R_{\mathrm{info}}^\star(B)
}_{\text{controller regret}}.
$$


Interpretation:
- No oracle advantage: little stopping opportunity exists in those trajectories under the allowed information.
- Oracle advantage, learned controller failure: estimation or optimization is the bottleneck.
- Advantage disappears after controller overhead: the computational benefit is not operationally useful.
- Advantage exists only for a label-aware hindsight oracle: useful exit variation exists, but may not be observable at deployment.


For a globally trained model-family claim, matched-budget fixed-depth models must also be trained separately. A frozen-backbone oracle test diagnoses one representation, not every possible representation.


### 5. Five-year bet


I would fund **calibrated continuation-value prediction, paired with task-specific certificates where available**.


The program would have three priorities:


| Priority | Why fund it | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Predict future task-loss improvement from current information | Directly targets the stopping decision | Miscalibration under distribution shift |
| Develop verifiable residuals or task certificates | Can provide stronger stopping guarantees | Limited task coverage |
| Evaluate adaptation using oracle gaps and full compute accounting | Separates representation, observability, and controller failures | Expensive evaluation |


I would not make the primary bet on:
- Larger collections of mutually competing gates.
- A universal state-motion measure of completion.
- Temperature schedules chosen from increasingly large sweeps.
- Maximizing difficulty–depth correlation.
- Claiming alignment from aggregate KL.


### Final assessment


The reported results are consistent with **misaligned objectives, weakly identifiable stopping signals, and self-reinforcing optimization failures**. They do not establish chaos, and they do not falsify adaptive computation.


The central scientific target should be:


$$
\boxed{
\text{Predictable, calibrated marginal task benefit per unit of additional compute.}
}
$$


That quantity is learnable under assumptions, certifiable in restricted settings, and impossible to recover universally from label-free geometry alone.


**Suggested next step:** run the matched-budget oracle-gap experiment before another hyperparameter sweep; it determines whether the missing ingredient is useful deeper computation, observable stopping information, or a controller capable of exploiting it.
